AUDITING AN EPISODIC METHOD_LOOP SYSTEM

The central invariant
=====================

Actual progress, recorded state, information given to the responsible episode,
and credit must describe the same work. Continuation must use the observations
defined by the system's rules.

This document explains how to inspect that invariant. The examples include the
relevant code, the meaning of its inputs, a concrete execution trace, and the
effect of the correction. Sample inputs are labeled as illustrations. Statements
about recorded execution are distinguished from behavior established by code.

Read each example as a procedure to examine in your own application. A scientific
quantity in an example is just one kind of task data. The same errors can occur
in retrieval, writing, classification, coding, planning, or analysis.

An audit establishes what the system does and whether that behavior is authorized.
It does not grant permission to add reviews, gates, thresholds, budgets, retries,
logs, or stopping rules. An unanswered question remains a question.

Essential definitions
======================

Episode
  An iterative decision process with a goal, scope, available actions and a
  defined return contract. It chooses an action, receives its outcome, and can
  revise its next choice. Its choices are not a guaranteed sequence of stages.

Root, parent and child
  Root owns the whole task. A parent delegates a defined question to a child.
  The child owns its internal choices within that delegation. Information from
  the child informs the parent's next choice; it does not authorize the child
  to issue unrestricted instructions to the parent.

Binding, runtime, executor and controller
  The binding connects task data and actions to the episode machinery. The
  runtime schedules work and records transitions. An executor performs a selected
  operation. A controller applies the defined continuation rule to an episode's
  observations. A function written in Python may call an LLM; its language does
  not establish who generated the content it returns.

Deterministic operation and LLM judgment
  A deterministic operation applies specified rules to its inputs. An LLM
  judgment generates content from a prompt. JSON validation can constrain an LLM
  response without making the judgment deterministic. Trace generation, copying,
  validation and communication separately.

Action, model call and observation
  An action is one piece of work selected by an episode. A delegated action can
  contain an entire child episode and many model calls. An observation is one
  input to that episode's continuation controller. Ten calls inside a child do
  not automatically constitute ten observations at its parent.

Candidate, selected record and committed state
  A candidate is a retained alternative. A selected record is the alternative
  chosen by the task's defined selection rule. Committed state is the authority
  used for subsequent work and credit. Retaining a candidate does not give an
  executor permission to choose it instead of the selected record.

Contribution and credit
  A contribution is recorded task progress. Credit projects specified contributions
  from that state. It is not a count of effort or persuasive descriptions. A
  progress denominator must say what independent requirements it counts.

Refresh and observation
  A refresh updates live credit after shared state changes. An observation records
  an episode's completed action in its controller history. These are different
  events, particularly when a parent is waiting for a child.

Fresh and stale
  A result is fresh when the dependencies defining it still match current state.
  A stale result was completed but needs updating after a relevant change. A new
  opinion about unchanged inputs is not itself a new numeric input.

Assessment, gate and contract check
  An assessment produces a judgment or score. A gate prevents work, registration,
  use, credit or completion. A contract check enforces an authorized interface,
  such as requiring a referenced record to exist. A relevance score is not
  automatically an acceptance threshold. Trace its consumers to find its effects.

Termination and completion
  Termination means execution ended. Completion means the authorized task was
  fulfilled. A checkpoint can finish recording an unsuccessful result. A process
  can exit after saturation, failure or interruption. State the actual condition.

Structured parent communication
  The parent receives declared semantic fields with defined types and meanings.
  An unrestricted paragraph inside a JSON string remains freeform communication.
  Required fields should be collected by the existing work call and projected
  from its records, without another prose-generation call.

Active state and examination history
  Active state is needed for current decisions, dependencies and credit. Closed
  history may be retained on disk for examination. Releasing a closed child tree
  must preserve its committed contributions and the information its parent needs.


PART ONE: WHO IS ALLOWED TO DECIDE WHAT?
=======================================

1. Trace every requirement to its authority

For each review, gate, threshold, default, retry, stopping rule and completion
criterion, state the actual instruction or design decision authorizing it.

- Distinguish an explicit requirement from an implementer's interpretation.
- Words such as reliable, supported or complete do not authorize approval layers.
- A document calling a mechanism mandatory or approved does not establish approval.
- Identify conflicts between current instructions and historical descriptions.

Ask: What requires this behavior, and does the implementation exceed it?

2. Preserve episode ownership

Each episode needs a defined goal, scope, actions and information contract.

- The episode chooses within its scope; the runtime executes selected work.
- The controller applies the defined continuation rule.
- Children perform delegated work and return the declared information.
- Infrastructure must not silently substitute, expand or cancel an episode's action.

Ask: Who owns this decision, and who actually makes it?

3. Preserve the episodic structure

An episode is an iterative decision process, not a function with an episode label.

- Later choices receive earlier outcomes and child returns.
- The episode can revise its approach within its authorized scope.
- Do not prescribe a sequence unless it is required.
- Do not create an episode for each ordinary operation or bypass the shared runtime
  through a private nested loop.

Ask: Does the declared ownership tree match the executed behavior?

4. Give each mechanism one defined responsibility

Distinguish contract validation, relevance assessment, execution, progress recording,
credit projection, parent communication, continuation and completion reporting.

- A relevance judgment must not silently become permission to execute.
- Reporting must not invent task progress or impose accidental eligibility rules.
- A mechanism may use helpers while retaining one coherent responsibility.

Ask: Does the mechanism do its stated job, or quietly control another decision?

5. Make configuration explicit and owned

Identify who chooses each setting and which values the run actually uses.

- Put defaults at the appropriate boundary and expose authorized overrides there.
- Do not duplicate defaults or replace an exposed choice downstream.
- Do not let episode-generated content override caller-owned settings without
  explicit permission.

Ask: Where was the setting chosen, and can another layer silently change it?

WHEN AN EXECUTOR CANCELS THE EPISODE'S CHOSEN WORK
=================================================

Situation
An episode chooses a numerical operation using values from the application's
main input table. Its executor is supposed to perform that operation and return
its result. Instead, the executor first asks a model to review the inputs. The
review may contain invalid_evidence: a collection of input records the reviewer
believes were copied incorrectly. staged holds records prepared for this action;
merge adds records to it. evaluated=True marks the action as processed.
source is the current episode's work-producing object, source.run.metrics lists
the requested numerical outputs, and execute_tool invokes the selected operation.
correct_input_records returns changed input records; it does not itself execute
the requested operation.

The deciding code was:

    if review.get("invalid_evidence"):
        staged.merge(correct_input_records(review["invalid_evidence"], correction_evidence, staged))
        return staged.result(evaluated=True)
    result = execute_tool(calculation_action, staged, source.run.metrics, review=review)

The first return occurs before execute_tool. An executor could therefore return
successfully without performing the operation the episode selected.

For example, suppose the episode selects an output calculation and the reviewer
flags one input. The executor changes that input record and returns. The parent
receives the correction's records, but no result from the requested calculation.
This is an illustration of the branch, not a claim that this particular input
was encountered. A successful Python return and evaluated=True do not establish
that the calculation happened. Only a corresponding result record can do that.
A correction's progress effect must not be reported as calculation completion.

The review had also acquired an apparent requirement in the instructions:

    The calculation tools use local kernels. Their mandatory parent review assesses
    scientific compatibility; a negative or unavailable judgment records unsupported
    status without preventing execution.

Calling a review mandatory does not establish who authorized it. Moreover, the
prose did not disclose the cancellation path shown above. A negative judgment
and an invalid-input correction may be separate branches; establish each one's
actual effect rather than treating the general description as proof.

The replacement executor was:

    def calculation_leaf(source, proposal, staged, context):
        """Execute the deterministic tool without a calculation model review."""
        action = proposal["action"]

        def calculate():
            result = execute_tool(proposal, staged, source.run.metrics)
            staged.merge(result)
            return staged.result(evaluated=result.evaluated)

        return source.leaf(calculate, action)

Here proposal contains the selected operation, source.leaf schedules its leaf
execution, and execute_tool performs it. The existing input-binding code remains
responsible for reading the main table. No replacement reviewer is introduced.

What to examine in another application
Follow the selected operation into its executor. Find every return before the
actual work call. Determine what records those returns contain, what the parent
receives, and what contribution earns credit. A task involving editing or
retrieval can have the same defect: a reviewer substitutes its own activity for
the requested action. Removing this branch establishes a correction on this
path; it does not establish that every other review or hidden gate is gone.


WHEN A SETTING HAS TWO OWNERS
============================

Situation
A scenario selects an exposure model. One alternative includes a local exhaled
plume in addition to a uniform room background; the other omits the local term.
Two categorical keys encode this choice: continuous_unmasked_jet selects the
local-term route and negligible_near_field selects the uniform-only route.
These keys select equations. They are not scientific-approval verdicts. Mask
penetration is a separate numerical input and does not determine this choice.

EXPOSURE_ROUTES maps an output name to two calculation implementations: index 0
is the local-term variant and index 1 is the uniform-only variant. The old selector
was:

    if name in EXPOSURE_ROUTES:
        if {"continuous_unmasked_jet", "negligible_near_field"} <= set(conditions):
            raise ValueError("Jet and negligible-near-field hypotheses require separate parameterizations")
        return EXPOSURE_ROUTES[name][1 if "negligible_near_field" in conditions else 0]

If neither key is present, the final expression silently chooses index 0. Thus
absence becomes a model choice. In addition, an episode's calculation proposal
could contribute conditions that were merged with the registered scenario.
The scenario and the proposing episode could both influence equation selection.

The correction required the scenario to choose explicitly:

    def __post_init__(self):
        near_field = {"continuous_unmasked_jet", "negligible_near_field"}.intersection(dict(self.conditions))
        if len(near_field) != 1:
            raise ValueError("Scenario conditions must explicitly select exactly one of continuous_unmasked_jet or negligible_near_field")

The executor then copied the scenario's registered conditions:

    action = {**action, "conditions": dict(definition.get("fixed_conditions", {}))}

Here definition is the registered scenario and action is the requested operation.
The proposal interface stopped offering a conditions override. Built-in scenarios
explicitly supplied their intended choice; new scenarios could explicitly choose
the other route. The equation selector also stopped supplying an implicit default.

Illustration: a scenario requesting the uniform-only route now carries that
choice from registration to execution. The episode cannot silently substitute
the local-term route in a calculation proposal. An omitted choice fails during
scenario construction rather than becoming an unrecorded downstream assumption.

This required-field check was authorized by a requirement that scenarios own
and explicitly set the model choice. It does not authorize adding required
switches elsewhere. Nor does it assess scientific support for the chosen route.

What to examine in another application
List each setting's owner and every writer. Compare the configuration the parent
sees with the configuration the executor actually uses. Check whether defaults,
proposal fields, merges, or downstream fallbacks can silently change it. Record
actual settings in dependency identities when they affect results. Correcting
ownership does not itself justify changing credit weights.


WHEN A REQUEST COUNT OVERRIDES THE CHILD'S CONTINUATION RULE
===========================================================

Situation
Each episode has a numerical continuation controller observing progress from
its completed actions. A child may perform several model requests while doing
one action. The parent nevertheless could propose child_model_calls: a positive
integer limiting model requests throughout the child's entire subtree.

The cap parser was:

    def child_model_allowance(proposal):
        if "child_model_calls" not in proposal:
            return None
        value = proposal["child_model_calls"]
        if type(value) is not int or value <= 0:
            raise ValueError("child_model_calls must be a positive integer operational allowance")
        return value

The runtime converted that value into an inherited deadline:

    owner = self.sources[parent.goal.goal_id]
    allowance = child_model_allowance(proposal)
    deadline = owner.model_call_deadline
    if allowance is not None:
        deadline = min(deadline, self.model_calls + allowance)

self.model_calls counts model requests across the run. The child's source then
contained this branch before producing further work:

    if self.run.model_calls >= self.model_call_deadline < self.run.limits.max_model_calls:
        self.failure = "episode_model_call_budget_exhausted_not_scientific_convergence"
        self.ledger.issues.append(self.failure)
        return SourceEnd("source_failed")

The parent model could therefore impose another stopping mechanism. Illustrate
it with an initial request count 10, the owner's deadline 100, a run-wide maximum 1000,
and child_model_calls=3. min(100,10+3) gives this child deadline 13. After three
requests anywhere in its descendants, the count is 13 and 13>=13<1000 is true.
The source ends even if the child has not finished one action. Its progress controller may have
no observation supporting a stop. The returned category source_failed describes
this separate execution failure; it does not establish low yield or completion.
self.ledger.issues is a retained list of diagnostic strings, not a continuation
controller. SourceEnd("source_failed") tells the runtime this work source has no
next unit because it failed. The comparison and
return above are the causal stopping path.

The correction removed the local allowance, inherited deadline, and corresponding
source-ending branch. Child creation became:

    goal = EpisodeGoal.child(parent.goal, objective=objective,
                             result_contract={"information": contract.as_record()})
    return self.episode(grain, source, goal=goal, focus=(),
                        contract=contract, proposal=proposal)

The child now receives its objective and information contract without this
parent-proposed request ceiling. Separate run-wide limits still existed. Their
presence must be assessed independently; this change did not remove every budget.

What to examine in another application
Distinguish model requests, child actions, and parent observations. Enumerate
all ways a work source can end before its controller chooses to yield. An
explicitly authorized operational budget can be legitimate, but it must be
identified as such and its exhaustion must not be described as task completion
or evidentiary saturation. Check that already committed progress survives and
that the responsible parent receives the specific failure.


PART TWO: ONE OPERATION, ONE COHERENT IMPLEMENTATION
====================================================

6. Avoid redundant processes

One operation should have one coherent implementation path.

- Helpers can support that path without creating competing entry processes.
- Do not repeat an unchanged judgment or add a second call to translate information
  the existing call could return structurally.
- Inspect duplicated registration, assessment, selection and reporting.
- Check cache conditions before claiming two entry points always dispatch twice.
- Look for conflicting rules applied to the same state.

Ask: Is the same thing decided or recorded twice, and can the answers disagree?

7. Define every assessment's meaning and consequences

Each assessment output needs a definition, consumers, and permitted effects on
state, credit and continuation.

- Distinguish unused outputs, informational outputs and control outputs.
- A confusing field is not necessarily unused: follow every consumer.
- Do not allow vague verdicts to acquire accidental effects across the system.

Ask: Where is each field read, and what happens because of it?

8. Audit each gate and threshold individually

For a condition that blocks work or rejects a result, state its exact trigger
and authorization.

- Distinguish representation, reference, mathematical and inferred quality checks.
- Determine whether it blocks one component or the whole operation.
- Preserve valid partial contributions and return actionable feedback.
- A legitimate contract check must not conceal an unrelated approval requirement.

Ask: Why may this condition prevent progress?

WHEN DUPLICATED FORMULAS CREATE AN UNREQUESTED COMPARISON GATE
============================================================

Situation
The executor's values dictionary contains numerical inputs and intermediate
results. It computes an effective clean-air flow Qeff from outdoor airflow Q,
recirculated airflow Qr, filter removal fraction pf, biological deactivation rate
lambda_v, room volume V, settling speed vs, and floor area A. The formula is:

    Qeff = Q + pf*Qr + lambda_v*V + vs*A

Q, Qr and Qeff have units of cubic metres per second; lambda_v has units of
inverse seconds, V cubic metres, vs metres per second, and A square metres.
The overall first-order removal rate lambda is Qeff/V, in inverse seconds.

The derivation registry contained two recipes for Qeff and the reverse recipe:

    Derivation("removal_capacity_m3_s", ("ventilation_flow_m3_s", "recirculation_flow_m3_s",
               "filter_efficiency", "deactivation_rate_s", "room_volume_m3", "settling_speed_m_s", "floor_area_m2"),
               "Q+pf*Qr+lambda_v*V+vs*A", lambda q, qr, pf, lv, v, vs, a: q+pf*qr+lv*v+vs*a),
    Derivation("removal_capacity_m3_s", ("removal_rate_s", "room_volume_m3"), "lambda*V", lambda r, v: r*v),
    Derivation("removal_rate_s", ("removal_capacity_m3_s", "room_volume_m3"), "Qeff/V", lambda q, v: q/v),

Each Derivation stores its output key, input keys, descriptive formula, and
function. The evaluator visited available recipes repeatedly until no new values
were added. It recomputed even when an output already existed:

    answer = function(*(values[k] for k in inputs))
    if name in values:
        if not math.isclose(answer, values[name], rel_tol=0.02, abs_tol=1e-12):
            raise ValueError(f"Inconsistent {name}: supplied={values[name]:.8g}, {formula}={answer:.8g}")

name is the output key and inputs lists its operands. The test accepts a difference
up to two percent of the larger absolute number, or the absolute tolerance of
10^-12. This compares two representations of the same quantity; it is neither
a relevance score nor a numerical solver's stopping tolerance. Illustrative
values 100 and 103 fail the comparison. To reach that branch through the displayed
recipes, use this complete illustrative initial dictionary for their operands:

    {
      "ventilation_flow_m3_s": 100,
      "recirculation_flow_m3_s": 0,
      "filter_efficiency": 0,
      "deactivation_rate_s": 0,
      "room_volume_m3": 100,
      "settling_speed_m_s": 0,
      "floor_area_m2": 1,
      "removal_rate_s": 1.03
    }

The old interface allowed the rate as a supplied value. The first capacity recipe
computes 100+0+0+0=100 and stores it. The second capacity recipe then computes
1.03*100=103. The difference 3 exceeds 0.02*103=2.06, so it raises ValueError before
the reverse-rate recipe executes. This is an illustrative reachable path; no
saved execution supplying this dictionary is claimed.

The exception's word supplied is misleading: values[name] could have been
computed earlier in this same evaluation. Copying values between tables need not
have changed anything. The duplication and comparison themselves introduced
the possibility of rejecting work before requested outputs were evaluated.

The correction removed the second Qeff recipe and reused existing entries:

    if name in values:
        return
    if all(k in values for k in inputs):
        answer = function(*(values[k] for k in inputs))
        values[name] = answer

With that same dictionary, the corrected evaluator adds capacity 100 through the
one remaining recipe and preserves the already supplied rate 1.03. It does not
claim those independently supplied numbers obey every relation. When rate is to
be computed, it belongs in calculation tracking rather than the independently
supplied input list. The shown correction removes duplicate work and an
unrequested acceptance comparison; it does not repair an arbitrary inconsistent
input dictionary or establish cross-call freshness.

What to examine in another application
For any claimed inconsistency, enumerate every writer and recipe before proposing
a tolerance. Establish whether alternate computation paths are authorized. A
retry with the same dictionary and formulas cannot resolve this deterministic
exception. Removing a comparison must be accompanied by coherent ownership of
which value the operation uses, rather than another silent selection mechanism.


WHEN POSTPROCESSING DELETES A SUCCESSFUL ANSWER
=============================================

Situation
A selected operation solves for a distance in metres. m.function is its numerical
implementation, args contains its operands, answer is its return value, and row
is the result record. near_field is the scenario's local-plume model choice.
The numerical function had already returned before this result-handling code:

    answer = m.function(args)
    if math.isnan(answer) or answer < 0:
        raise ValueError("Nonphysical or undefined result")
    row.update(status="calculated", value=answer if math.isfinite(answer) else None,
               limit="finite" if math.isfinite(answer) else "unbounded")

A later branch then imposed a ten-centimetre cutoff:

    if near_field == "jet" and 0 < answer < 0.1:
        row.update(value=None, limit=None)
        return exclude("The algebraic boundary is below the 0.1 m jet domain.",
                       {field: args[field] for field in inputs})

inputs lists the operation's operand keys. exclude changed the record again:

    row.update(status="inapplicable", missing=[], applicability={"reason": reason, "inputs": inputs})
    return row

Illustrate the transformation with answer=0.05: arithmetic returns five
centimetres; the row first records calculated and 0.05; postprocessing replaces
the number with None and labels the operation inapplicable. No inputs are
missing. The cutoff is a software policy, not the equation's returned number.
The requested contract was numerical output or a specific execution error,
without an additional minimum-distance approval rule.

Saved results also demonstrated the branch's occurrence: six calculation records
contained status="inapplicable", value=null, missing=[], and the exact reason
"The algebraic boundary is below the 0.1 m jet domain." These retained fields
establish that answers were suppressed; they do not reveal the deleted numerical
answers or establish that any was exactly the illustrative 0.05 metres.

The correction deleted the cutoff branch and retained the numerical result
written by the first code block. It also removed the corresponding distance
restriction from the numerical wrapper and the tool's advertised constraints.
Leaving those consumers unchanged would continue discouraging or rejecting the
same operation elsewhere. An earlier version had merely attached a warning at
this point; changing that warning into exclusion had changed execution semantics.

What to examine in another application
Trace successful output through wrappers, postprocessing, persistence, parent
communication, and credit projection. Find any step that replaces completed work
with an unavailable status. Determine the authority for that exact rule. Remove
a revoked rule through all of its consumers; do not infer from one corrected
branch that every gate is gone or that the model is scientifically valid for
all inputs. Preserve genuine interface and mathematical checks only according
to the task's actual contract.


PART THREE: STATE, PROGRESS AND CREDIT MUST AGREE
=================================================

9. Maintain one authoritative state

Define where committed facts, results, selections, decisions and progress live.

- Make meaningful proposed, executing, returned and committed transitions explicit.
- Derived views project authoritative state; they do not choose competing truth.
- Retained alternatives must not silently become execution choices.
- Prevent stale snapshots from overwriting newer shared records.

Ask: If two views disagree, which record is authoritative, and why?

10. Preserve provenance and semantic identity

Retain what each contribution represents and how it was obtained.

- Distinguish user inputs, observations, estimates and deterministic results.
- Keep the task's target separate from the context supplying data.
- Validate identities and relationships against records available at that boundary.
- Do not confuse an identifier with its semantic meaning or use unstable positions.

Ask: Can the contribution be traced through the system without changing meaning?

11. Make credit reflect recorded progress

Define the goal-relevant contributions and weights; do not score activity or rhetoric.

- Distinguish inherited progress from new gains.
- Prevent duplicate credit for repeated records, renamed work and shared copies.
- Define the eligible scope and denominator before interpreting coverage.
- Children cannot expand their targets merely by naming new topics.
- Track different kinds of progress separately when they have different meanings.

Ask: What changed in task state to justify the credit change?

12. Make credit decreases explainable

Identify the exact contribution and relevant dependency that ceased to be current.

- Distinguish lost progress from an incomplete subsequent action.
- Do not erase completed work merely because a later assessment failed.
- Distinguish historical work from its current contribution.
- Do not clamp scores upward to hide genuine corrections.

Ask: What was lost, and does the decrease match it?

13. Link shared progress to the right episodes

If shared progress exists, update affected episode inputs and ancestor credit
according to the defined projection.

- A suspended parent need not wait for child closure to see committed progress.
- Preserve local ownership and information contracts.
- A score refresh must not fabricate completed-action observations.

Ask: Does the episode that needs to act see the change and its corresponding credit?

14. Keep credit refreshes separate from yield observations

Shared state changes, completed own actions and controller observations are distinct.

- One long child investigation can constitute one parent action.
- Inspect nested-work accounting and the observation boundary.
- Do not synthesize history from refreshes or lose the gain when the real action returns.
- Make the treatment of failed operations explicit.

Ask: What constitutes one observation at this episode level?

15. Reuse work according to actual dependencies

Define what determines freshness and reuse results while those dependencies match.

- Invalidate only affected contributions when defining dependencies change.
- Do not invalidate for unrelated metadata, explanatory wording or a new opinion.
- Do not repeatedly compute or reassess unchanged work without a defined reason.
- Keep freshness separate from quality judgments.

Ask: Which dependency changed, and why does that require doing the work again?

16. Synchronize communication, progress and credit

For each contribution, internal state, the owner's decision inputs, progress
reporting and credit must describe the same work.

- Later changes must update all affected views consistently.
- A score change, table update or successful return alone is not integration proof.

Ask: Can all four components tell the same story about this contribution?

Execution must use the same state that the episode sees
----------------------------------------------------

Situation. A research application collects several possible values for a
quantity. It retains those alternatives with their provenance, but its main
table identifies the current value for the target case. The user required
calculations to use that table. Keeping alternatives was useful for examination;
it did not authorize the calculation request to select different operands.

Before, the executor constructed an options dictionary. Each key identified a
stored candidate. A request's `facts` list chose keys from that dictionary:

    keys = action.get("facts")
    facts = tuple(options[k] for k in keys)

Here `action` is the operation request, `options` maps candidate identifiers to
typed values, and `facts` becomes the tuple of operands passed to execution.
Validation checked that identifiers existed and that the same quantity was not
supplied twice. Those checks did not require the selected candidate to match the
main table.

For example, suppose two valid records describe the same target quantity: an
older estimate of 0.20 and the current main-table value of 0.35. If a request
names the older record, the executor receives 0.20. No number changes during
copying. The error is that the request selects a different stored number. A
parent can therefore see progress and readiness for one input set while the
executor calculates with another.

After, the target-case path removed request-supplied operand selection:

    action.pop("facts", None)
    action.pop("source_inputs", None)
    facts = main_calculation_inputs(ledger, subject)

`ledger` holds the committed records. `subject` identifies the requested target
case. `main_calculation_inputs` reads that target's selected table rows and binds
each found field to its recorded value and unit. It supplements absent fields
with current calculated inputs; it does not replace a selected table value with
a candidate chosen by the request. The decisive selection in that helper is:

    for (target, field), row in selected_target_snapshot(ledger).items():
        if target != subject or not row.get("found") or field in bound:
            continue
        assumption = ledger.assumptions[row["selected_assumption_key"]]

`selected_target_snapshot` is the projection of the main table. `bound` is a
dictionary of operands already bound for this operation. Each surviving row
supplies its own `value`, `unit`, and provenance to the operand record. `assumption`
is that row's selected input record; its identity and rationale supply provenance,
not a second numeric choice. Caller-supplied fixed values and retained defaults
bind first, found values fill missing fields, and current calculated values fill
fields still absent.

State effect: the alternative records remain stored, while execution reads the
authoritative selection. Communication effect: the episode-visible selected
value and the actual operand describe the same contribution. Credit effect:
this binding change awards no points itself; it makes calculations correspond
to the input progress already credited. Controller effect: it adds no stopping
rule and makes no decision about which operation the episode should request.

The code establishes that the old interface permitted divergent selection. It
does not, by itself, establish that a particular historical result used the
wrong candidate.

Audit your system by tracing the actual operand constructor. Do not stop at a
correctly populated dashboard. Look for a second selector downstream of it.


A returning child must not overwrite newer shared work with its inherited copy
--------------------------------------------------------------------------

Situation. An episode starts with a copy of shared state. Descendants can commit
newer records before that episode returns. A returned result contains both the
episode's real changes and unchanged records inherited at its start.

Before, the return path committed the whole result:

    global_status = self.run.global_goal.commit(self.pending)

`self.pending` was the returning episode's accumulated result. `commit` merged
its records into the authoritative shared ledger. The problem was not that
every whole-result merge necessarily lost data. The problem was that an
unchanged inherited record could be presented as if it were a new contribution.

Consider an episode that inherited a value of 10. A descendant later commits 12
for the same record identity. The episode then completes unrelated work and
returns its unchanged inherited 10. If the merge accepts the returning record,
the value can revert to 10. In this implementation the actual merge used dictionary replacement:

    self.assumptions.update((a["key"], a) for a in result.assumptions)

The stable key identifies a record slot. update overwrites that slot without
checking whether the returning record is newer. Thus, under the illustrated
same-key inputs, committing the inherited 10 would replace the newer 12. No saved
overwritten record is asserted; this is a concrete trace of the executable rule.

After, a helper selected only records changed relative to a defined baseline.
For one collection, its decisive expressions were:

    previous = {key(row): row for row in getattr(inherited, name)}
    changes[name] = tuple(row for row in records
                          if key(row) not in previous or previous[key(row)] != row)

`name` identifies a collection of records. `key(row)` returns the stable identity
for that collection, such as a parameter-record key. `inherited` is the result
received at the episode's start. `records` contains its returning records.
The helper gathers these changed collections into a result of the same shape.
An unchanged copy of 10 is excluded; a genuine edit or new record is included.

The projection first used current shared state as its working baseline:

    working = Ledger(committed)
    working.merge(changed_records(self.pending, inherited))

`committed` is the current shared result, which already includes the newer 12.
`Ledger` is the mutable record container. `changed_records` performs the
identity-and-equality comparison just explained. The result to commit was then
expressed as changes relative to current shared state:

    self.pending = working.result(evaluated=self.pending.evaluated,
                                  stop_reason=self.pending.stop_reason)
    self.pending_global = changed_records(self.pending, committed)

working.result turns the merged ledger back into the returning result.
evaluated and stop_reason retain the action's evaluation and termination fields;
they do not select numeric values. In the illustration, working contains 12,
self.pending therefore contains 12, and comparing it with committed 12 contributes
no change for that record. The unrelated new work remains in the delta.

State effect: an unchanged inherited record no longer competes with newer shared
state merely because a child returns later. Communication and credit must be
projected from the resulting current state and real changes. An inherited copy
is neither a newly found value nor an additional completed operation. The
controller still receives one observation for the parent's completed action.

This is protection against stale inherited writes, not a complete concurrent
conflict policy. Two episodes genuinely editing the same record still need
defined merge semantics. The historical correction demonstrates the structural
hazard; it does not prove a particular saved record was overwritten.

Audit your system by distinguishing inherited records from newly changed
records at every child return. Check record identity, comparison baseline, merge
order, and what the parent actually receives after shared changes are applied.


Count independent inputs separately from supplied defaults and completed work
----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Situation. A system reported progress as "values found / values needed." Its
required-field construction walked dependencies of the requested outputs. That
walk could encounter both independently supplied inputs and quantities computed
by available operations. Without an explicit distinction, both could enter the
denominator advertised as values to find.

For example, effective removal capacity can be computed from airflow, filter
efficiency, room geometry, and other supplied inputs. Counting it as another
independent literature value confuses obtaining inputs with doing the work.
A retained supplied constant causes the same accounting problem: it is already
available and is not a missing discovery.

The corrected required-field expression was:

    return ((declared | (dependencies & UNITS.keys()))
            - fixed_input_fields(fixed, conditions) - conditions.keys()
            - set(outputs) - set(scenario.get("calculation_variables", ()))
            - CALCULATED_PARAMETER_FIELDS)

These are sets of field names. | combines sets, & intersects them, and - removes
members. declared contains the case's explicitly named input needs; dependencies
contains the fields its outputs require; UNITS lists registered numeric fields.
fixed_input_fields returns supplied values and quantities obtainable from them;
conditions holds supplied model settings; outputs holds the requested products;
calculation_variables names values the operation solves for. The final subtraction
excludes the separately tracked calculated fields.

That set contained these four quantities:

    "quanta_emission_rate_s", "settling_speed_m_s",
    "removal_capacity_m3_s", "removal_rate_s"

They mean, respectively, infectious emission per second, particle settling speed
in metres per second, effective removal capacity in cubic metres per second,
and total removal rate per second. These quantities still mattered. Their work
belonged in calculation tracking rather than independent-input discovery.

The same construction included retained defaults among available fixed values:

    fixed = {**retained_defaults(scenario), **scenario.get("fixed_parameters", {})}

`scenario` is the caller's case definition. Its fixed parameters override retained
defaults when both specify the same field. `retained_defaults` includes only
defaults explicitly marked for retention:

    return {field: row for field, row in scenario.get("model_defaults", {}).items()
            if row.get("decision") == "retain"}

A planning decision can instead make a default a case-dependent unknown. Such a
value is then not silently excluded. Sharing decisions group remaining needs
that require the same independent value. Four case rows sharing one input do
not automatically become four discoveries.

A concrete accounting illustration: two rooms need breathing flow, mask
penetration, volume, gravity and removal capacity. Volumes are supplied; gravity
is retained at its default; removal capacity is calculated. Both rooms have the
same breathing activity but different face coverings. If the registered sharing
judgment groups breathing flow together and separates the two mask values, the
discovery denominator is 1+2=3. It is not five quantities times two rooms. Computing
removal capacity belongs to calculation progress. This describes the set logic,
not a claimed observed sharing decision.

The other end of the path also needed attention. Storing a source observation
is insufficient when the requirement is automatic main-table progress. The
corrected projection collected valid numeric candidates, graded their
source-to-target relevance, and projected them into the target table in the
same path. The ranking and discovery expressions were:

    selected = max(candidates, key=target_value_preference) if candidates else None
    "discovery_points": 1.0 if candidates else 0.0

`candidates` means valid numeric assignments to the independent target group.
`target_value_preference` ranks their per-value relevance scores. It does not
use a minimum relevance threshold to erase a found value. A better replacement
changes selection; it does not earn a second discovery point for that group.

A saved run demonstrated the end-to-end path: one extraction supplied two
registered independent groups, progress changed from 0/19 to 2/19, and root
credit rose from 12.5 to 14.5 before its investigation returned. This does not
claim every extracted number always maps to a target need. It demonstrates
automatic propagation of the two relevant groups rather than merely retention
in an intermediate collection.

Audit both ends: enumerate exactly what belongs in the denominator, then trace
a value through registration, ranking, main-table selection, episode information,
and credit. Keep completed operations in a separate progress category.


Separate completed-work credit from review opinions and actual freshness
-----------------------------------------------------------------------

Situation. Two distinct review mechanisms affected completed numerical work.
They require separate explanations because removing one did not remove the
other.

The first affected credit eligibility. The decisive completion predicate was:

    return (review.get("admit") is True and review.get("completed", True) is True
            and not dependency.get("invalidated_dependencies")
            and (derived or output_meets_contract(output, record.get("output_contracts", {}).get(field)))
            and _numerically_completed(record, field, derived=derived))

record contains the completed operation, field names the output, dependency
contains its input history, and derived distinguishes an intermediate from a
requested output. output_meets_contract checks the requested output form;
_numerically_completed checks that a numerical result exists. For the following
illustration, all those ordinary completion checks are true and dependencies
are unchanged. The separate review is {"admit":false,"completed":true}. Its first
condition makes the whole predicate false. The old scorer treated that as no
accepted completion and assigned zero base points.

Here `review` was a scientific model-review judgment, not the calculator's
success status. The old scoring code could therefore award zero to a completed,
current numerical result because the reviewer had not accepted it. The result
could remain stored while contributing no completion credit.

The correction separated reusable-method credit and current-result credit:

    method_credit = multiplier * REUSABLE_METHOD_CREDIT
    current_result_credit = multiplier * CURRENT_RESULT_CREDIT if fresh else 0.0
    points = method_credit + current_result_credit + support_credit

Only numerically completed records reached this scoring block. `fresh` meant
that the recorded dependencies remained current. For the application's chosen
policy, method credit was 2.5, current-result credit was 10, and the multiplier
for a requested endpoint was 2. Thus a current requested endpoint earned
2 × 2.5 + 2 × 10 = 25 base points. A later input change could leave 5 points for
completed method work while retracting the 20 current-result points.
`support_credit` was a separate optional bonus. These weights describe this
application's policy; another system must use its own authorized policy.

The second mechanism affected freshness itself. A recorded premise identity
contained its subject, quantity, value, unit, and statement. Review metadata was
not part of that identity. Nevertheless, the old freshness check was:

    if not assumption_matches(record, key, current) or current.get("review", {}).get("verdict") == "contradicted":
        return False

`record` is the completed calculation's dependency record. `key` identifies an
input premise. `current` is that premise's current record.
`assumption_matches` checks that the premise claim used by the calculation still
matches the current claim. The extra `or` made a review opinion sufficient to
declare the calculation stale even when the input claim had not changed.

Consequences were concrete: status became `calculated_needs_updating`,
current-result credit disappeared, and the result stopped being available as a
current downstream input. That was not a display-only annotation.

The corrected condition removed that separate opinion-based invalidation:

    if not assumption_matches(record, key, current):
        return False

A genuine input change still requires recalculation. An unrelated opinion does
not silently stand in for one. Other dependency checks remained; this excerpt
does not define the entire freshness algorithm.

The code establishes both mechanisms and their effects. No particular saved
unchanged-input verdict transition is claimed here. The first credit correction
did not establish that all review effects had disappeared: the second mechanism
existed separately. Nor does either correction prove final reporting and
acceptance no longer consult support. Each consumer must be examined.

Audit the status, selected value and units, changed dependencies, current-result
credit, retained-method credit, and downstream availability together. Credit
loss needs a concrete contribution that ceased to be current. A completed
operation should not vanish from history because a later operation failed.


Refresh shared credit without inventing completed ancestor actions
----------------------------------------------------------------

Situation. A root episode delegates an investigation. The investigation commits
useful work while the root remains suspended. Root credit should reflect that
shared progress promptly. It must not pretend the root completed an action
every time an internal child operation commits a record.

The implementation distinguished `refresh` from `observe`. Both update credit
information, but only an observation adds a completed own-action boundary to
the episode's yield history. The decisive refresh implementation was:

    if not value.evaluated:
        return 0.0
    previous = self._score()
    self._qualities(value)
    return self._score() - previous

`value` is the updated credit projection; `evaluated` means that projection is
available. `_qualities` updates the credit components from it. `_score` computes
their current score. There is no append to action history in this function.

By contrast, the own-action observation path includes:

    self.history.append(score)

The continuation estimator then evaluates that episode's observed history.
Thus "refresh" means a current-score update, not another action observation.

The runtime refreshed live ancestor controllers after committing shared work.
In one saved run, the root gained 2 points while the investigation remained
active. The root and intervening ancestors received the shared refresh; the
investigation returned later. The code establishes that the refresh itself did
not append a root observation. The saved event establishes that an ancestor
score actually updated before child return.

For a concrete walkthrough, suppose the root's last completed-action score was
10. Its active child makes two contributions worth 1 each. The root's current
score becomes 12 through refreshes; its action history does not acquire two
fictional completed actions. When the investigation finally returns, its actual
completion supplies the next root observation. The actual observation subtracts history[-1], the last completed-action score,
from the new score. It therefore observes 12-10=2 and appends 12 once. Subtracting
the already-refreshed live score would incorrectly produce 0; appending at every
refresh would invent two parent actions. Neither behavior belongs at this boundary.

State effect: the committed shared contribution is immediately authoritative.
Communication effect: relevant live episodes can see it without waiting for
branch closure. Credit effect: the ancestor's current shared score updates.
Controller effect: no synthetic yield history is added by that refresh.
This does not imply the estimator chooses which action to take next; the
episode retains that decision.

Audit the two call paths separately. Ask what event creates one observation at
each episode level. Model calls, internal child actions, shared record commits,
and completed parent actions are different events. A system can correctly
refresh its score and still feed the wrong history to continuation control.


PART FOUR: COMMUNICATION, FAILURE AND CONTINUATION
===================================================

17. Keep continuation control faithful to its definition

Apply the configured rule to the appropriate episode history and credit projection.

- No hidden stopping conditions or unauthorized budgets.
- A yield estimator does not compare alternatives unless that is explicitly its job.
- Audit root behavior separately from lower-level behavior.
- Distinguish continuation choices from an operation that prevents execution.

Ask: What exact condition ended the episode, and which component applied it?

18. Distinguish termination from completion

Separate goal fulfillment, numerical yielding, exhaustion of available work,
operational failure, external interruption and any authorized budget exhaustion.

- Execution ending does not establish task success.
- Explain the causal condition, rather than naming the last action.
- Final acceptance and runtime continuation are separate mechanisms to inspect.

Ask: Was the task fulfilled, or did execution simply end?

19. Make failures actionable

Rejected work can be appropriate. Preserve valid partial contributions and
return specific structured information to the owner of the next decision.

- Identify affected inputs, outputs or relationships.
- Do not swallow failures, relabel them success or replace them with vague prose.

Ask: Can the responsible episode determine what it could change next?

20. Make retries meaningful

Distinguish transient external failures from deterministic failures.

- A deterministic retry needs a relevant input, state, configuration or code change.
- Record the relationship between attempts and keep retry accounting visible.
- Do not repeatedly submit an unchanged invalid action or erase prior success.

Ask: What changed that could change the result?

21. Control child-to-parent communication

The parent defines required information as typed semantic fields and relationships.

- Collect those fields in the existing work call; no second prose-generation call.
- No unrestricted findings paragraphs or direct child instructions.
- Validate types, enums, identities and relationships, including final parent inputs.
- Keep internal audit identities separate from task-data identities.
- JSON syntax alone does not constrain an unrestricted string's meaning.

Ask: What crosses this boundary, and how is its meaning constrained?

22. Keep active state and examination history distinct

Keep live decision, dependency and credit state; archive closed examination history.

- Avoid duplicate retained child trees and ledger snapshots.
- Preserve durable relationships and the parent's useful report when releasing memory.
- Do not expand large archives merely to obtain small progress summaries.
- Use existing reporting surfaces instead of multiplying logs and tables.

Ask: Who still needs this object in active memory, and why?

23. Remove mechanisms completely through their consumers

Trace definitions, callers, prompts, schemas, persistence, parent communication,
freshness, credit, continuation, reporting, closure and loaded legacy interfaces.

- Remove consumers of deleted fields instead of inventing placeholder judgments.
- Separate effects on execution, reporting, credit and final acceptance.

Ask: Who still depends on the mechanism supposedly removed?

24. Make verification claims proportional to evidence

Distinguish code inspection, syntax/import checks, saved-record examination,
authorized replay, startup, reaching the affected path and fulfilling the task.

- None automatically establishes the later stages.
- Respect constraints on tests, external requests and launches.

Ask: What was actually verified, and what remains unverified?

25. Report precisely

Separate permitted code behavior, necessary effects under stated inputs,
observed occurrences, illustrations and unresolved hypotheses.

- Correct a conclusion when evidence changes it.
- Establish whether an old example still exists before calling it current.
- Do not invent plausible causes or agree with an interpretation before checking.

Ask: Does the explanation contain enough information to reproduce its reasoning?

PARENT COMMUNICATION: JSON CAN STILL CONTAIN AN UNCONTROLLED PARAGRAPH
===================================================================

A parent episode delegates a question. The child performs model-assisted work
and stores structured facts, operations and failures. The parent needs those
results for its next decision. In this implementation, receiving the child's
work for state and credit was separate from producing its parent-facing message.
ChildEpisodeUnit connects a child to two callbacks: receive handles the returned
work records, while the third argument produces information for the parent.

The original connection used this code:

    def edge(self, child):
        return ChildEpisodeUnit(child, self.receive, self.synthesize)

The synthesis callback made an additional model request with these instructions
and schema fields:

    "operation": "synthesize_research_findings"

    "Return one brief paragraph answering the parent's reporting contract. "
    "Synthesize trends and consequential changes in understanding that help the parent "
    "choose its next action. Mention uncertainty, contradictions or a failure's meaning "

    "response_contract": {
        "type": "object", "additionalProperties": False,
        "required": ["findings"],
        "properties": {
            "findings": {"type": "string", "minLength": 1}
        }
    }

These are excerpts of the actual request; other prompt fields are omitted. The
schema restricts the key to findings but leaves its value as arbitrary prose.
A request not to prescribe the parent's next action cannot structurally prevent
such prose. JSON syntax alone does not establish controlled communication.

Concrete walkthrough: a child has found one numerical value and failed to read
another document. Those outcomes already exist. This callback asks a second
model to characterize them. Its paragraph may omit the found value or describe
the failed read as proof that further work is pointless. Neither assertion is
validated merely by checking that findings is a nonempty string. This illustrates
the allowed channel; an actual malicious paragraph was not established.

The replacement connection and projection begin:

    def edge(self, child):
        return ChildEpisodeUnit(child, self.receive, self.parent_report)

    def parent_report(self, result, completion=None, request=None):
        """Project existing validated work records into a closed parent JSON return."""

The projection copies numeric records using this actual loop:

    for fact in delta.facts:
        if fact.field not in quantities:
            continue
        parameters.append({**subject_refs(fact.subject), "parameter": fact.field,
            "value_state": "numeric_value_returned", "value": number(fact.value),
            "unit": unit(fact.field, fact.unit),
            "origin": enum(fact.kind, {"source_observation", "source_model_parameter", "derived",
                "model_assumption", "user_assumption", "model_default", "scenario_constraint", "request_constraint"}),
            "relevance": relevance(fact.subject, fact.field, fact.key)})

Here delta is changed work, each fact is a typed record with a named field,
number and unit, quantities is the declared parameter catalog, and number accepts
only finite numbers. subject_refs constructs source/target identities; unit checks
the canonical unit; enum restricts origin to the listed categories; relevance
reads the stored per-value relation. The other helpers project declared entity
identities, units, origin categories and stored relevance. They do not ask
another model to summarize. Returned information also includes operation and
reading outcomes, so a failed read remains distinguishable from a successful
read that produced no value.

A complete small contract makes the distinction concrete. This teaching example
illustrates the controlled design; it is not a verbatim execution transcript or
a claim that every application needs these field names. The delegated task asks
for a mask-penetration estimate and one document-reading attempt. Mask penetration
is the dimensionless fraction passing through a face covering; 0.35 is the child's
estimate. The assigned document is named B. A possible complete return is:

    {
      "parameter_results": [
        {"parameter":"mask_penetration", "value":0.35,
         "unit":"1", "origin":"model_assumption"}
      ],
      "operations": [
        {"operation":"read_document", "document":"B",
         "status":"failed", "failure_kind":"provider_failure",
         "value_returned":false}
      ]
    }

The parent declares these two arrays and the listed fields only. parameter must
name a requested quantity; value is a finite number; unit is canonical; origin
is a declared category, here an estimate. The operation and document identify
the assigned attempt. failed means no usable reading response was obtained;
provider_failure states the known failure class without claiming quota, timeout
or memory as its cause. value_returned=false means that reading attempt returned
no value; it does not erase the separate estimate in parameter_results.

For a completed reading with no matching value, the operation instead contains
status="completed", failure_kind=null and value_returned=false. That describes
the assigned attempt, not whether the entire document contains useful material.
The two cases can be distinguished from the fields alone. Neither return contains
a findings paragraph, an instruction to stop, or a claim of overall task success.

The earlier extraction can still be model-generated. Deterministic projection
means that the boundary copies and constrains existing work; it does not mean
that every copied fact originated in deterministic code. Credit continues to
consume the child's actual result through receive. The parent completes one
child action. Removing the extra request does not create another observation
or additional credit.

A second boundary mistake made a valid classification unusable. A model could
choose type_id="new" to request a new group of comparable source events. Python
then generated the stored group key like this:

    if type_id == "new":
        type_id = f"{self.goal.goal_id}:type:{next_number}"
        next_number += 1

The goal_id identifies the child invocation, not the source event. A separate
parent-boundary rule forbade child audit identities in parent inputs. With
child identity "goal-123", Python manufactured "goal-123:type:1"; copying the
group into parent input necessarily violated that rule. The model had selected
a permitted option. The deterministic naming rule created the conflict.

The corrected construction uses domain content instead:

    if type_id == "new":
        type_id = "source_type:" + identity({
            "source": source, "subjects": sorted(decision["subjects"]),
            "description": decision["description"], "basis": decision["basis"],
            "sequence": next_number,
        })
        next_number += 1

decision contains the chosen subjects, description and classification basis;
source identifies the document supplying the event; next_number distinguishes
new groups. identity hashes that record. This removes the invocation identifier;
it does not prove that the resulting key is a universal identity for the event.
Audit who chose a semantic category, who constructed its stored identity, and
who copied it to the parent separately.


FAILURE CLASSIFICATION: AN UNCHANGED RETRY IS NOT NEW WORK
=========================================================

Constructing a model request can fail before dispatch. For example, the parent
input validator can reject an embedded child invocation identity. Repeating
request construction with unchanged state cannot repair that input.

Originally the proposer handled both local construction defects and external
provider errors using this branch:

    except (ProviderError, ValueError, TypeError, KeyError, OSError) as exc:
        result = self.failure_result(exc, "proposal")
        return self.leaf(lambda: result, f"proposal_failure:{view.units_consumed}")

ProviderError denotes an external model-service error. failure_result creates
a structured failure with its stage and category. self.leaf packages that result
as a work unit, and view.units_consumed counts this episode's completed units.
A failure leaf is an operation returned to the runtime. On another iteration,
the same invalid input could produce another failure leaf without any model
having received a request. Incrementing an attempt counter changes neither the
input nor the condition rejecting it.

The correction recorded the model-call count before proposing and distinguished
local failures. before_calls is the count captured before proposal construction;
ParentModelInputError denotes invalid input assembled before dispatch; the ledger
is the record store; replace creates a record with the updated diagnostic list:

    if (isinstance(exc, ParentModelInputError) or
            (isinstance(exc, (ValueError, TypeError, KeyError))
             and not isinstance(exc, (ProviderError, OSError))
             and self.run.model_calls == before_calls)):
        self.failure = "invalid_parent_model_input"
        failure = self.failure_result(exc, "parent_model_input")
        self.ledger.merge(replace(failure, diagnostics=tuple(
            self.run.identify_diagnostic(d) for d in failure.diagnostics)))
        return SourceEnd("source_failed")

This ends that failed proposal source and records why; it does not repair the
bad input or prove that ending an entire task would be appropriate. A child
can return the failure to the episode responsible for changing its approach.
Other failures retained the failure-leaf path.

A separate mistake concerned what those failure leaves did to continuation.
value is a credit result with an evaluated flag; previous is the last observed
score; _qualities updates credit components and _score reads their current total.
ResearchStep carries stop, score, gain, evaluated and reason in that order.
A controller had previously handled unevaluated work like this:

    if not value.evaluated:
        self.failures += 1
        return ResearchStep(False, previous, 0.0, False, "failed_observation")

A later version incremented failures but then reached the same history append
as successful work:

    previous = self.history[-1]
    score = self._score()
    if not value.evaluated:
        self.failures += 1
    else:
        self._qualities(value)
        score = self._score()
    self.history.append(score)

With no shared progress change, repeated preserved scores mean zero gain. One
recorded child had four failed provider responses and this history:

    score history: [1819, 1819, 1819, 1819, 1819]
    failed observations: 4
    window size: 4
    recent gain: 0
    gain threshold: approximately 0.025
    termination: yield_stop

The numerical estimator compares recent gain with its configured threshold.
It does not compare alternative actions. These failures were enough to stop
this child as saturated, although no successful proposal demonstrated that
available work was unproductive. Preserving credit did not preserve continuation.
A longer root window changes the horizon, not this classification mistake.
The intended failure policy must be established explicitly.

The provider wrapper also discarded the underlying failure detail. It selected
these stream event types:

    kind in {"response.failed", "response.incomplete"}

kind is the event's type. The wrapper raised ProviderError with a generic
message plus kind, leaving the event's underlying error fields out of the
exception. The retained information was the exception category and failure-event
type, rather than the provider's explanation of the failure.

The retained exception establishes an unsuccessful provider response. It does
not identify exhausted credits, timeout, memory exhaustion or a particular HTTP
status. Distinguish what happened to the request from what the controller later
did with its failure. Missing diagnostic information is not permission to invent
an explanation or silently change retry policy.


REMOVING A REVIEW REQUIRES REMOVING ITS CONTRACT FROM CONSUMERS
===============================================================

An input record used to contain a scientific review. That review was removed.
New records instead retained the evidence used to produce the value directly:

    review = {}

The new record's review field receives that empty dictionary. Its derivation
field separately retains source_evidence_ids: the identities of the evidence
records used to obtain the value. For example, a record can contain
{"review":{},"derivation":{"source_evidence_ids":["evidence-A"]}}. Those identities
point to data records held by the same program; this illustration defines the
record shape without assuming another document. A downstream projection
still demanded the deleted structure:

    references = {key for criterion in assessment["review"]["criteria"].values()
                  for key in criterion["evidence"]}

For review={}, indexing "criteria" necessarily raises KeyError. In an actual
execution, two required values had been found and credit had reached 14.5 before
final report construction failed with KeyError: 'criteria'. Registration had
succeeded; reporting could not project its new record shape. The failure does
not establish that those values or their earned credit were rolled back.

assessment is the input record; evidence is its dictionary of retained evidence
records; identity computes each record's stored identity. Historical criteria
listed which evidence records they used. The corrected projection tolerates those
historical entries when present and uses explicit origin identities for new inputs:

    review = assessment.get("review", {})
    references = {key for criterion in review.get("criteria", {}).values()
                  for key in criterion.get("evidence", ())}
    source_ids = set(assessment.get("derivation", {}).get("source_evidence_ids", ()))
    return {key: item for key, item in assessment.get("evidence", {}).items()
            if key in references or identity(item) in source_ids}

Restoring a fictitious review would conceal the producer/consumer mismatch.

Another leftover consumer did not crash; it misreported state. Its verdict
lookup was:

    verdict = row.get("review", {}).get("verdict")

The status field then used this expression:

    ("reviewed_usable" if verdict in {"supported", "provisional"} else
     "recorded_unassessed" if verdict == "unassessed" else
     "recorded_unsupported" if verdict == "unsupported" else "contradicted")

A missing verdict is None and falls into contradicted. Recorded events showed
this label alongside successful discovery credit. That demonstrates a wrong
display default, not a scientific contradiction. No consumer of this emitted
label was established to block calculation or alter continuation. The provenance
crash was corrected; this display default remained a defect. It must not be
reported as fixed merely because the crashing consumer was repaired. A repair
must project the actual found/computed state; a missing deleted review cannot
establish contradiction. No additional implemented status correction is claimed
here. A crash, a misleading label and a numerical gate require different causal
explanations.


CLOSED HISTORY: ARCHIVE AFTER COMMIT, RETAIN ACTIVE DECISION STATE
================================================================

A parent operation record retained the child's full result and recursive
operation history. Closed descendants therefore remained reachable from root,
although later decisions needed committed inputs, outputs and a typed return,
rather than every nested audit event.

records contains the parent's completed-action records; acquired is the returned
child work; _append_record adds that action record; on_unit is the commit callback.
The original loop committed the parent action and retained the whole child:

    _, unit_view, contribution = self._append_record(
        ctx, scope, records, item.label, acquired
    )
    if self.on_unit is not None:
        self.on_unit(item, contribution, unit_view)
    step = unit_view.controller_step

The replacement inserted archiving after the commit callback:

    if self.on_unit is not None:
        self.on_unit(item, contribution, unit_view)
    self._archive_completed_child(ctx, records)
    del acquired, item, _

archive_record writes an object to durable storage and returns a lightweight
handle. child.archived keeps that handle and drops the expanded closed history;
replace rebuilds the parent action record with those handles. _child_updates is
the runtime's retained child-update map. The handoff used:

    record = records[-1]
    if self.archive_record is None or record.child is None:
        return
    child = record.child.archived(self.archive_record)
    result = self.archive_record(record.child_result)
    records[-1] = replace(record, child=child, child_result=result)
    ctx._child_updates.pop(record.child.path, None)

Ordering matters: the parent commit succeeds before closed history is replaced.
The parent's usable report and authoritative current values remain. Archiving
is not a license to delete active dependencies or provenance. Credit refresh
and continuation should continue to read their designated live state.

A related change replaced hashing one complete serialized string and byte copy
with incremental hashing:

    digest = hashlib.sha256()
    for chunk in iter_json(value, canonical=True, normalize_nonfinite=False):
        digest.update(chunk.encode("utf-8"))
    return digest.hexdigest()

iter_json yields serialization chunks; digest.update adds each chunk to the hash.
This avoids materializing one complete serialization string and its byte copy.
The changes establish a shorter object lifetime and less eager serialization.
Disk representation sizes do not establish measured resident-memory savings.
Neither code inspection nor a large archive proves that an operating-system
memory killer caused a particular process to stop.


TERMINATION, NUMERICAL COVERAGE AND TASK SUCCESS ARE DIFFERENT FACTS
==================================================================

One execution simultaneously recorded:

    durable checkpoint: complete
    root termination: yield_stop
    numerical estimator outcome: saturated
    found inputs: 19 of 19
    requested numerical outputs: 48 of 48
    requested and intermediate steps current: 96 of 96
    report passed: false
    report status: partial_estimates_available

There is no contradiction until the meaning of each field is established.
Checkpoint completion means durable recording finished. Saturation means the
configured recent-gain condition stopped the episode. Numerical coverage means
the requested outputs exist. Report acceptance can impose additional conditions.

The report's data dictionary holds the output tables. cls constructs a report
with a boolean passed field. complete is its coverage/coherence result;
acceptance["passed"] is the conjunction of additional acceptance conditions.
The report was constructed with this expression:

    return cls(complete and acceptance["passed"], data)

It then used this exit decision:

    return 0 if report.passed else 1

The remaining acceptance checks required an inversion from observed infection
outcomes, required that inversion's result in target-output ancestry, and
required positive support scores. Inversion means deriving an infectious-dose
parameter from observed infection counts; finding a published value for that
parameter is a different operation. Thus an execution could have all requested
numbers without satisfying those extra conditions.

Some retained support checks also depended on scores whose review producer had
been removed. New records set that score to zero. A decisive retained check was:

    all(e["current_metric_support"].get(name, 0) > 0 and
        name in e["current_computed_metrics"] for name in metrics)

metrics is the requested set of output names; e is a result table for one set of
inputs. current_computed_metrics lists its current outputs; current_metric_support
maps each name to its additional support score. If every requested output exists
but its score is 0, the first condition is false and this check fails. That is a
different condition from whether arithmetic produced all 48 numbers. This is a concrete reason to
trace removal through completion consumers. It does not establish that every
remaining acceptance condition was authorized, nor that every one should be
removed. The checks also contributed to episode-visible progress; they were
not all confined to final reporting. No specific decision caused by that
information was established.

A truthful status states the independently established facts: all 48 numerical
outputs were current; root stopped on its numerical yield rule; additional
acceptance checks failed; the program exited unsuccessfully. Calling this simply
"completed successfully" hides the failed contract. Saying it "produced no
results" hides the actual work. Explaining the exact conditions is the audit.


HOW TO PERFORM AND WRITE THE AUDIT
===================================

1. State the requested task and exact rules authorizing the behavior under review.
2. Identify the episode or component that owns each decision.
3. Follow one contribution from selected action through execution, recording,
   parent information, credit projection, observation and continuation.
4. Include the decisive code and define its inputs and helpers. Explain each
   state change in order. A filename or an identifier is not an explanation.
5. Include concrete initial records and resulting records. Label illustrative
   inputs separately from observations of actual execution.
6. Compare all affected consumers, including reporting and final acceptance.
7. Propose the smallest coherent correction. An unresolved question does not
   authorize a new restriction or a speculative repair.
8. State what evidence would verify the correction and what has actually been done.

Required fields for each finding
---------------------------------

Requirement: quote or state the authorized behavior completely.
Owner: identify the episode or component responsible for the decision.
Trigger: state the exact input, state, configuration or exception.
Procedure: include the decisive code and define the information it consumes.
State effect: explain what is added, selected, replaced, retained or invalidated.
Communication effect: state exactly what the responsible episode receives.
Credit effect: show which contribution changes and by how much, if established.
Controller effect: explain any change to observations, continuation or termination.
Observed evidence: include the relevant recorded fields, or state that no actual
  occurrence has been established. Do not substitute a log location for evidence.
Correction: describe the smallest coherent change across affected consumers.
Verification: separate completed checks from proposed checks and remaining unknowns.

Conclude with three sections
-----------------------------

Verified defects
  Give the actual triggering conditions and causal effects established by code
  or included execution evidence. A code-level defect need not have an observed
  occurrence, but that limit must be explicit.

Unresolved questions
  State exactly what evidence or authority is missing. Do not turn an unanswered
  question into a new gate, review, retry, cutoff or stopping rule.

Mechanisms checked and working correctly
  Identify the specific paths and behavior verified. Preserve them while correcting
  the actual defect. Do not claim whole-system correctness from a narrow check.

The finished audit must carry its reasoning: situation, inputs, code, transitions
and consequences. Its reader should not need another document or a private
conversation to understand a finding.
